Statistical report: aspirin and heparin in acute ischaemic stroke

Satwik Kotta · Reanalysis of the International Stroke Trial (IST) · Dashboard · Code (R, SAS)

1. Objective

Reproduce the primary results of the IST from the public patient level data, then extend them: a covariate adjusted estimate of the aspirin effect, a benefit and harm comparison by heparin dose, survival models, subgroup tests with multiplicity correction, sensitivity analyses for missing outcomes, and a validated prognostic model.

2. Trial and data

The IST randomised 19,435 patients within 48 hours of suspected acute ischaemic stroke, in 467 hospitals in 36 countries, between 1991 and 1996. The design was 2 by 2 factorial: aspirin 300 mg daily or avoid aspirin, and subcutaneous heparin at 5,000 IU (low) or 12,500 IU (medium) twice daily, or avoid heparin, for 14 days. The primary outcome was death or dependency at 6 months. The database (version 2, University of Edinburgh, 2011) has one row per patient and 112 variables.

3. Data checks

  • 19,435 patients, no duplicate records, arms balanced in size (9,720 aspirin, 9,715 no aspirin).
  • The pilot coded medium dose heparin as H (245 patients). Merged with M, as the database notes direct.
  • 6 month outcome: 53 patients coded 8 or 9 (unknown) and 97 coded 0, a code the documentation does not define. All 150 treated as missing, leaving 19,285 (99.2%) with a known outcome.
  • Atrial fibrillation was not recorded for 984 pilot patients. They are kept, as their own level, in adjusted models.
  • 420 patients had a final diagnosis of not a stroke. They stay in the main analysis, as randomised, and are excluded in a sensitivity analysis.
  • Baseline factors are balanced between arms: every standardised mean difference is under 0.025.

4. Methods

  • Analysis set. Intention to treat. Because the aspirin by heparin interaction was not significant (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.13), each drug is compared across the other's arms.
  • Primary outcome. Crude risk difference with the Newcombe hybrid score interval and a chi squared test. Adjusted risk difference by standardisation: a logistic model with age, delay and systolic blood pressure as natural cubic splines, sex, conscious level, stroke subtype, atrial fibrillation, visible infarct, CT before randomisation and the other factorial arm; predicted risks averaged under each arm; 400 replicate bootstrap interval.
  • Early events. Death, recurrent ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, major extracranial bleed, and death or recurrent stroke within 14 days, per 1,000 patients, for aspirin and for each heparin dose against no heparin.
  • Survival. Kaplan Meier to 183 days, and a Cox model with the same adjustment. Proportional hazards checked with Schoenfeld residuals.
  • Subgroups. 8 baseline factors, each with a likelihood ratio test for interaction, Holm corrected across the 8.
  • Missing data. Multiple imputation by chained equations (20 imputations, logistic imputation model including the 14 day outcomes, pooled by Rubin's rules), and worst and best case bounds.
  • Prognostic model. Logistic regression on baseline factors, 5 fold cross validation, AUC and calibration slope.

The crude aspirin result matches the published 62.2% against 63.5% (2p = 0.07) exactly. Adjusting for baseline prognosis narrows the interval, as expected in a large trial with strong prognostic factors, and gives 1.5 fewer dead or dependent patients per 100 (odds ratio 0.92, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.99), close to the published adjusted estimate of 1.4 per 100.

Aspirin prevents about 9 recurrent ischaemic strokes per 1,000, with no clear rise in haemorrhagic stroke and about 5 more major extracranial bleeds. Heparin's preventive effect is matched or outweighed by bleeding, with a steep dose response in harm: medium dose causes about 13 haemorrhagic strokes and 17 major extracranial bleeds per 1,000. Neither dose changed death or dependency at 6 months (trend across dose, p = 0.68).

Survival. Adjusted hazard ratio for death by 6 months on aspirin 0.96 (0.91 to 1.02), p = 0.25. Proportional hazards held for aspirin (p = 0.20) but not for heparin (p = 0.02), consistent with early bleeding deaths, and not for several baseline factors, so risk differences are the main summary.

Subgroups. No interaction for aspirin was significant (smallest uncorrected p = 0.20). For heparin, stroke subtype gave p = 0.04 uncorrected, but 0.29 after Holm correction, so it is not evidence of a different effect.

Prognostic model. Cross validated AUC 0.777 with a calibration slope of 0.99: baseline factors predict outcome far more strongly than either treatment, which is why adjustment gains precision.

6. Conclusions

  • Aspirin started within 48 hours gives a small but real benefit, about 1.5 fewer patients dead or dependent per 100 at 6 months and 12 fewer deaths or recurrent strokes per 1,000 in 14 days. The result holds under multiple imputation and every sensitivity analysis except the most extreme missing data assumption.
  • Routine heparin gives no net benefit. Its reduction in recurrent ischaemic stroke is offset by haemorrhagic stroke and extracranial bleeding, which rise steeply with dose.
  • No subgroup shows a convincing difference in effect once multiple testing is accounted for.

7. Limitations

  • A 1990s trial, before thrombolysis and thrombectomy, so the results describe the drugs as tested then.
  • 33% of patients had no CT scan before randomisation, so some haemorrhagic strokes were randomised.
  • Open label design for the avoid arms; the 6 month outcome was assessed by postal or telephone follow up.
